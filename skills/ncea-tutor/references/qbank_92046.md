## 92046-2025-Q1b · 2025 Exam Q1(b) · Q1 total 8 marks · Merit/Excellence

- Location: 92046-exm-2025.pdf, Question One (b).
- Topic: why Wellington's average monthly temperature varies through the year.
- Answer: The Earth's 23.3° axial tilt means uneven solar radiation. When the Southern Hemisphere tilts towards the Sun it gets more (and more direct) solar radiation → summer, Wellington's warm months (e.g. January). Tilted away → less radiation → winter (coldest ~July). At the equinoxes the tilt is neither towards nor away, so radiation is more even. Excellence requires linking the monthly graph values to the solar-radiation argument.
- Verified: every point cross-read from the 2025 schedule's evidence text; the 23.3° tilt figure matches the paper. HIGH.

## 92046-2025-Q1c · 2025 Exam Q1(c) · Q1 total 8 marks · Merit/Excellence

- Location: 92046-exm-2025.pdf, Question One (c).
- Topic: why daylength changes through the year (sunrise/sunset data given).
- Answer: Earth spins once every 24 hours → the side facing the Sun has day. The 23.3° tilt changes the Sun's height in the sky through the year. In summer the SH tilts towards the Sun → Sun higher in the sky and stays up longer → longer days (6:01 a.m.–8:55 p.m.). In winter the SH tilts away → Sun lower, less time above the horizon → shorter days (7:45 a.m.–5:05 p.m.). Excellence links the day/night and Sun-height relationships together.
- Verified: all points cross-read from the 2025 schedule; the daylength change (14 h 54 min vs 9 h 20 min) recomputed from the paper's own table. HIGH.

## 92046-2025-Q2b · 2025 Exam Q2(b) · Q2 total 8 marks · Merit/Excellence

- Location: 92046-exm-2025.pdf, Question Two (b).
- Topic: why observers on Earth see different Moon phases.
- Answer: We see the Moon because sunlight reflects off its surface; one half is always lit. As the Moon orbits, the proportion of the sunlit half facing Earth changes: when the Moon is between Sun and Earth the shadowed side faces us (new moon); a quarter-orbit later half of the lit side shows (first/third quarter); opposite the Sun we see the fully lit face (full moon). Phases cycle crescent → quarter → gibbous → full. The hemispheres see the same phase but inverted.
- Verified: all points cross-read from the 2025 schedule's evidence text (reflection, half always lit, orbit, phase sequence, hemisphere inversion). HIGH.

## 92046-2025-Q2c · 2025 Exam Q2(c) · Q2 total 8 marks · Excellence

- Location: 92046-exm-2025.pdf, Question Two (c).
- Topic: why a super full moon is not observed every month.
- Answer: A full moon happens when Earth is exactly between the Sun and Moon — once a month, every ~29.5 days (the extra 2 days over the 27.5-day orbit are because Earth also moves around the Sun). Perigee (closest approach, largest apparent size) repeats every ~27.5 days, so the perigee and full-moon timings drift out of step and only occasionally coincide — a few times a calendar year.
- Verified: cross-read from the 2025 schedule; the 27.5 vs 29.5 day values match the paper's own statement. HIGH.

## 92046-2025-Q3b · 2025 Exam Q3(b) · Q3 total 8 marks · Merit/Excellence

- Location: 92046-exm-2025.pdf, Question Three (b).
- Topic: why Auckland (37°S) and Invercargill (46°S) have different daylengths.
- Answer: Definitions — equinox: Sun directly above the Equator, similar daylengths; winter solstice: Sun lowest in the sky, shortest day; summer solstice: Sun highest, longest day. As the year progresses the sunrise/sunset points move along the horizon (equinox: rises due E, sets due W; winter solstice: rises NE, sets NW; summer solstice: rises SE, sets SW). Auckland is closer to the Equator, so at the winter solstice it has the longer day; Invercargill is closer to the pole, so at the summer solstice it has the longer day. At an equinox both are similar.
- Verified: all points cross-read from the 2025 schedule's evidence text and consistent with the paper's stated latitudes. HIGH.

## 92046-2025-Q3c · 2025 Exam Q3(c) · Q3 total 8 marks · Merit/Excellence

- Location: 92046-exm-2025.pdf, Question Three (c).
- Topic: Sun angle and shadow differences between the Equator and Dunedin at the spring equinox.
- Answer: At the Equator the noon Sun is 90° (directly overhead) → maximum direct solar radiation and NO shadow. Dunedin at 46°S has a much lower noon Sun angle (43°), so the same radiation is spread over a larger area → weaker/less direct, and shadows are long. Shadow length grows as the Sun angle drops with increasing latitude.
- Verified: the 90° and 43° values cross-read from the paper's own table; the shadow/angle reasoning cross-read from the 2025 schedule. HIGH.
